Gridlocked · a puzzle-difficulty study

A bigger maze
isn't a harder maze.

We solved ≈14,000 Rush-Hour-style puzzles — two board shapes, four traffic densities — and measured what actually tracks difficulty. The obvious answer, how much room the puzzle has to move, predicts almost nothing. What the pieces demand of each other predicts nearly everything.

One red car. One exit. A handful of blockers. Every puzzle in the study is a variation on this.

The setup

Free the red car.

Slide blockers out of the way until the red car can reach the exit. Simple rules — but difficulty ranges from trivial to genuinely brutal, and we wanted to know why.

The intuition

Surely a bigger space is harder.

The instinct is that a puzzle with thousands of reachable arrangements must be tougher than one with a few hundred. We measured it across every puzzle type. The correlation was essentially zero.

total_states → ρ ≈ 0.0
What actually matters

How deep the chain goes.

The real work is nested prerequisites: to move the red car you must first move A, and to move A you must first move B, and B needs C gone. Dependency depth — the length of that forced chain — was the strongest predictor in every single puzzle type.

dependency_depth → ρ 0.61 – 0.83
And the twist

Sometimes you retreat to win.

Greedy play — always move toward the exit — is how humans get stuck. The puzzles that punish that instinct, where the winning move goes backward, score as measurably harder.

counterintuitive_frac → ρ 0.44 – 0.75

The longest chain of moves that must happen in order to free the target.

Measures how far ahead you must plan, not how many moves you make. Four cars that each independently clear the lane is depth 1. Four in a forced chain is depth 4 — same move count, far more thinking. Strongest predictor everywhere, and it got stronger as boards filled up.

The share of solution moves that make things look worse — away from the goal, or deeper into the jam.

Greedy distance-shrinking is exactly how people get stuck, so puzzles that force a retreat feel harder. We report the fraction, not the raw count — longer solutions trivially accumulate more.

optimal_solutionsρ 0.46 → 0.68

How many genuinely different shortest solutions exist — not reshuffles of one.

The surprise: harder puzzles have more, not fewer. A typical Rush Hour puzzle has a median of 484 distinct optimal solutions; almost none have just one. “There’s one clever path” is a myth — difficulty is the forest, not the needle.

The number the giant tangled state-graph shows off, and everyone’s starting intuition. Across two geometries and four densities it sat at essentially zero — sometimes negative. A vast space can hide an obvious solution; a cramped one can force a deep chain. Size is the wrong question.

The part nobody shows you

Three ways the average lied to us

Measuring puzzle difficulty is a minefield of confounds. Three times in one study a plausible statistic pointed the wrong way — each caught only by digging past the headline number. This is the honest spine of the story.

01

The metric that graded itself

deception_ratio = states ÷ moves. We correlated it against moves — the very thing it divides by. Its flat result wasn’t a finding, it was arithmetic eating its own tail.

02

The count that just measured length

Raw counterintuitive_moves scored a gaudy 0.93 — because longer solutions mechanically fit more of them. As a fraction of the path, the honest number is 0.44.

03

Simpson’s paradox in the depths

Pooled, unique- and many-solution puzzles look identical. Split by solution length, unique-solution puzzles are twice as deep at every length — the pooled average erased it.

These correlations are against optimal move count, an objective difficulty proxy — not yet how hard humans feel a puzzle is. That test needs human-labeled puzzles (the 40 tiered Rush Hour challenge cards are next).
