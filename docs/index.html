Gridlocked · a puzzle-difficulty study

A bigger maze isn't a harder maze.

We solved ≈14,000 Rush-Hour-style puzzles — two board shapes, four traffic densities — and measured what actually tracks difficulty. The obvious answer, how much room the puzzle has to move, predicts almost nothing. What the pieces demand of each other predicts nearly everything.

What actually matters

How deep the chain of prerequisites goes.

The real work isn't distance — it's order. To move the red car you must first move A; but A is pinned by B; and B needs C gone first. That forced sequence is the puzzle's spine.

And the twist

Sometimes you have to retreat to win.

Humans play greedily — every move should shrink the gap to the exit. The nastiest puzzles punish exactly that instinct: the only way forward is backward. Try the obvious move first.

The part nobody shows you

Three times the average lied to us.

Measuring difficulty is a minefield of confounds. Three plausible statistics pointed the wrong way — each caught only by digging past the headline number. This is the honest spine of the study.

01
The metric that graded itself — deception_ratio = states ÷ moves
We correlated it against moves, the very thing it divides by. Its flat result was arithmetic eating its own tail, not a finding.
02
The count that just measured length — counterintuitive_moves
A gaudy 0.93 — because longer solutions mechanically fit more of them. As a fraction of the path, the honest number is 0.44.
03
Simpson's paradox in the depths — unique vs many solutions
Pooled, they look identical. Split by solution length, unique-solution puzzles are twice as deep at every length. The average erased it.
These correlations are against optimal move count — an objective difficulty proxy, not yet how hard humans feel a puzzle. That test needs human-labeled puzzles; the 40 tiered Rush Hour challenge cards are next.
Square numbers are cross-validated — our solver matched an independent 2.5-million-puzzle reference on every sampled board. Hex numbers rest on our own solver alone; no external reference exists for the three-axis board.
